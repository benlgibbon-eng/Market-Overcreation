I  —   The Proposition
Executive Summary

A trophy asset for a market that has been waiting for one.

Saltwood & Stone will be Australia's most considered hinterland hotel — a sixty-key estate two hours south of Sydney that competes, in product and price, with Saffire Freycinet, qualia, and the Baillie Lodges portfolio.

The Berry-Jamberoo hinterland sits inside the country's most affluent travel catchment yet contains no offering above four stars. The drive-up market within ninety minutes exceeds four million people, and the inbound market — North American, North Asian, and European — has, since 2023, been actively seeking experiential luxury in regional New South Wales.

The estate will be designed as a private compound: a restored homestead at its heart; pavilion suites tucked into the paddocks; a destination restaurant, working farm, spa house, and twenty-five-metre pool pavilion. The interior language is drawn from Tamsin Johnson Interiors — lime-washed plaster, travertine, raw linen, unlacquered brass, and a curated layer of vintage European furniture set against Australian native landscape.

We are seeking A$155 million of project capital. Stabilised EBITDA is forecast at A$12.4 million by year three, with an exit valuation of A$220–260 million on a 7-to-10-year horizon.

II  —   The Place
Berry · Jamberoo · Kangaroo Valley

Soft country, three rivers, an escarpment, and the sea.

The South Coast hinterland is the closest thing Australia has to Tuscany or Provence — a thirty-kilometre band of green dairy country pressed between the Illawarra Escarpment and seven-mile beaches.

Berry is a town of antique stores, two-hat restaurants, an annual literary festival, and a population that has tripled in median income over the last decade. Jamberoo lies fifteen minutes north — quieter, set against Saddleback Mountain, with Minnamurra Rainforest on its doorstep. Kangaroo Valley is forty minutes inland, all stone bridges and waterfalls. Seven Mile Beach is fifteen minutes away.

The location offers what no other Australian luxury site can: a true drive market. Sydney is one hundred and forty kilometres north — a ninety-minute drive, or a thirty-minute helicopter transfer from the Eastern Suburbs. The South Coast has become, in the post-2020 era, the preferred weekend territory of the same Sydney households who built the holiday-house economies of Palm Beach and the Southern Highlands. They are now actively spending, and there is nowhere of the right calibre for them to stay.

Catchment

4.1m people within a 90-minute drive. Greater Sydney median household income for the target postcodes (2027, 2028, 2030, 2088, 2095) sits 2.4× the national average.

Access

90 min by car from Sydney CBD. 30 min by helicopter from Rose Bay. 25 min from Shellharbour Airport — direct daily services to Melbourne and Brisbane from 2025.

Climate

Mild oceanic. 248 sunny days per year. No cyclone or bushfire-extreme zone overlay. Year-round operating envelope — no closed season.

III  —   The Vision
Design Direction

An estate, not a hotel. Tamsin Johnson's hand, at scale.

The brief to the design team is unambiguous: Saltwood is a private estate that takes guests, not a hotel that imitates one.

The architectural mood is drawn from the working farms of the Illawarra and the agrarian villas of southern Italy. Long, low forms; deep eaves; lime-rendered walls in chalk and bone; standing-seam zinc roofs that have weathered to a matte silver. The hand of Tamsin Johnson Interiors runs through every room — antique Italian travertine on the floors, blackbutt and spotted gum beams overhead, vintage French and Belgian furniture set casually against custom pieces in oak and bouclé, art commissioned from Australian painters working in the same earthy register.

The palette is settled, not styled. Bone, oat, fawn, terracotta, faded olive, smoked oak. Unlacquered brass that will tarnish honestly. Limestone, hand-thrown ceramics, antique Berber and Anatolian rugs. Nothing shouts; everything has been chosen.

The landscape design — by an Australian practice with international hinterland experience — will lean on native grasses, lomandra, kangaroo paw, gum, and ironbark, with kitchen gardens and an olive grove in the central paddock.

IV  —   The Property
Schedule of Accommodation

Sixty keys, four typologies, one estate.

The site brief calls for a working hinterland property of 60 to 120 hectares — large enough to hold the building program without compromising the sense of arrival, with at least one ridge line offering escarpment-to-sea views and an existing watercourse or dam. Three candidate parcels have been identified in the Berry and Jamberoo cadastres; due diligence is underway on the preferred site.

The accommodation program is deliberately weighted toward the upper categories. Half of all keys offer plunge pools or outdoor bathing pavilions. Every key has a fireplace, a private terrace, and a view of either the escarpment or the paddocks. No key sits adjacent to another's terrace.

Amenities

The estate's social architecture is built around five public houses.

Stone & Salt

The destination restaurant. 70 covers in a converted cattle shed. A produce-driven menu under a chef-of-renown (target: a 2- or 3-hat Sydney operator opening their first regional kitchen). Open to non-resident guests on a limited basis.

The Drawing Room

The guest-only dining room and bar. Set within the restored homestead. Long-table breakfasts, all-day grazing, a fireside bar with a deep cellar of regional and European wine.

Bathhouse

The spa. Six treatment rooms, a 25-metre indoor pool, hammam and Nordic plunge sequence, vitality garden, and a partnership with an Australian botanical line. Resident wellness practitioner in residence.

The Garden

The working farm. Two hectares of kitchen garden, an olive grove, a small dairy, beehives, and a chef's table program with daily harvest walks. Supplies the restaurant.

The Pool Pavilion

The summer house. A 25-metre heated outdoor pool, cabanas, a daytime grill and bar, and an open-air cinema folded into the orchard for summer screenings.

Excursions

The concierge program. Helicopter transfers, escarpment walks, private surf instruction at Seven Mile, sailing from Greenwell Point, kayaking the Kangaroo Valley, riding on the estate's own horses.

The thesis, restated

The closest analogue in the world is not in Australia.

It is the Tuscan agriturismo at Borgo Santo Pietro, or Belmond's Castello di Casole, or Heckfield Place in Hampshire — working estates re-imagined as private hotels with a six-figure ADR and a waiting list. Australia has the geography, the affluent catchment, and the produce. It does not yet have the estate.

Saltwood is that estate.

V  —   Competitive Set

Where Saltwood sits in the Australian luxury map.

The advantage

Drive market. The only Australian property of this calibre that does not require a domestic flight. We capture two booking patterns simultaneously: the long-stay destination guest and the high-frequency Sydney weekender.

The scale

60 keys. Large enough to justify a destination restaurant, full spa, and resident programming. Small enough to retain genuine intimacy and exclusivity. This is the proven sweet spot — qualia, Cape Lodge, and Borgo Santo Pietro all operate at this scale.

The brand

Independent, with optional affiliation. Operated as a standalone brand with the option of soft affiliation (Leading Hotels of the World, Relais & Châteaux) to access international distribution without dilution.

VI  —   The Guest
Target Segments

Three guests — two domestic, one international.

I

The Sydney Weekender

40% of room nights

Eastern Suburbs and Lower North Shore households, 38–58, $750k+ HHI. Books two or three short stays a year, often midweek. Drives down with a partner, returns on Sunday afternoon. Spends heavily on F&B, spa, and the cellar.

Average stay: 2.4 nights · Repeat rate: 2.1× per year

II

The Domestic Destination Guest

35% of room nights

Melbourne, Brisbane, Perth, and Auckland travellers, 45–65, planning a milestone stay. Books a Villa or Escarpment Suite for four to seven nights, often around a celebration. Books experiences in advance. The qualia and Saffire crossover guest.

Average stay: 4.6 nights · Repeat rate: 1.3× per year

III

The Inbound Discoverer

25% of room nights

North American, North Asian, and European travellers, 40–70, often pairing the estate with a Sydney stay and a Tasmania or Outback leg. Found via Relais & Châteaux, Virtuoso, and the editorial press. Highest ADR cohort.

Average stay: 3.2 nights · Repeat rate: 0.4× per year

Ancillary revenue

Resort guests at this tier spend approximately 70% of room revenue on ancillary services. The estate is designed to capture that spend on-property: a 70-cover destination restaurant, two bars, the spa, retail and wine programs, private events, and experience packages. Modeled non-rooms revenue at year three: A$14m.

Returns & exit

The base case assumes 70% senior debt at 7.0%, 30% equity. Stabilised EBITDA of $12.4m supports a debt service of approximately $7.6m, leaving $4.8m of free cash to equity in year three rising to $7.5m by year five as the property matures and ADR compounds at 3–4% annually.

Australian trophy resort assets have transacted at 18–24× stabilised EBITDA over the last cycle (Saffire, qualia, Capella Lodge, Lizard Island). At 18×, exit valuation is A$223m; at 22×, A$273m.

Target equity IRR of 14–16% over a seven-to-ten-year hold, with a 2.4× equity multiple in the base case.

Risk register, summarised

Planning risk. Mitigated by early heritage and traffic assessment, low-density paddock pavilion typology, and active local engagement. Three candidate sites maintained until DA in hand.

Construction risk. Fixed-price head contract with a tier-one regional builder; contingency of 8% within the $155m budget; staged drawdown.

Demand risk. Mitigated by drive-market exposure (less sensitive than fly-in resorts to international cycle), independent brand with affiliation optionality, and three diversified guest segments.

Operating risk. Pre-opening engagement of an experienced GM (target: a senior alumnus of Baillie Lodges, Aman, or COMO) twelve months ahead of opening.

Climate risk. Site selection avoids bushfire-extreme and flood-prone overlays. Building envelope designed to BAL-29; all guest pavilions are detached masonry-and-zinc.

IX  —   The Ask
Capital Structure

A$46.5 million of equity, alongside committed senior debt.

We are inviting a club of three to five aligned investors to subscribe alongside the founding sponsors for the A$46.5m equity tranche.

The capital structure is: 70% senior debt ($108.5m) on a 10-year term at an indicative 7.0% all-in cost, supported by stabilised cover of 1.5×; 30% equity ($46.5m), of which the founding sponsors will subscribe for approximately one-third. The remainder is being placed with strategic limited partners — family offices and institutional capital with a hospitality or coastal real-asset thesis.

The structure includes a 6% preferred return to equity, an 8/20 promote above the hurdle, and standard tag-along, ROFO, and pre-emption rights at exit. Minimum ticket: A$2.5m.
